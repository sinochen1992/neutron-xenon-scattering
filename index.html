Explore elastic scattering

Start at DD energy, 2.45 MeV, with natural xenon. Both evaluated libraries start selected. Move the compound slider to 0.10 or 0.24 and watch the orange curve change while the evaluated curves stay fixed.

What is being plotted?

A cross section measures the likelihood of a specified interaction, expressed as an effective area. One barn (b) is 10⁻²⁸ m². “Total” includes every interaction; “elastic” is one part of it. In elastic scattering the neutron and xenon nucleus keep their identities and the nucleus ends in its original internal state. The nucleus can still gain recoil energy.

Non-elastic means all other final states: an excited xenon nucleus (inelastic scattering), capture with gamma emission, and other energetically open channels such as emission of additional neutrons or charged particles. Which channels are open depends on isotope and neutron energy. Inelastic scattering is included in the accounting, but these plots show elastic recoils only.

σtotal = σelastic + σnon-elastic

σelastic = σshape + σcompound elastic

σoptical reaction = σcompound elastic + σnon-elastic

The last two relations describe the energy-averaged bookkeeping used by this spherical-model illustration. Here σtotal = σshape + σoptical reaction. Setting fraction f assigns fσoptical reaction to compound elastic and leaves (1−f)σoptical reaction as non-elastic. It repartitions a fixed total. The slider does not add anything to the evaluated libraries.

For example, the direct spherical calculation for natural Xe at 2.45 MeV gives about 3.633 b of shape elastic and 2.195 b of optical reaction. With f = 0.10, 0.2195 b is assigned to compound elastic: elastic becomes about 3.853 b and non-elastic about 1.975 b. Their sum remains about 5.828 b. The display grid introduces small interpolation differences.

Why “optical,” “potential,” and “shape”?

The neutron behaves as a wave. The optical model describes its average interaction with a nucleus using a complex potential U = V + iW, analogous to refraction and absorption of light. The real part changes the wave's phase and direction; the imaginary part removes flux from the explicitly described elastic wave. Both parts affect elastic scattering.

Shape elastic or potential elastic is the elastic scattering calculated from that potential. Optical reaction measures the flux missing from this coherent elastic wave. Some of that flux can form a compound nucleus and later return to the elastic final state. Therefore optical reaction is not automatically the same as the physical non-elastic cross section. Compound elastic describes that formation-and-decay mechanism; it is not another name for inelastic scattering.

The assumed compound term here is isotropic in the centre-of-mass frame: dσcompound/dΩ = σcompound/(4π). Actual compound emission need not be perfectly isotropic. Statistical decay models, such as Hauser–Feshbach calculations, are needed to predict its size and angular distribution.

The four main plots

  • Upper left — angle: dσ/dΩ is cross section per unit solid angle, in b/sr. θ is the neutron's centre-of-mass scattering angle. Blue is shape elastic; dashed orange is shape plus the assumed compound term. Deep dips arise from wave interference. The two evaluated curves already contain each library's complete elastic prediction.
  • Upper right — recoil: the same elastic events are expressed as xenon recoil energy ER, in keV. For one isotope, ER = Emax(1−cos θ)/2 and dσ/dER = (4π/Emax) dσ/dΩ. Head-on backscattering gives Emax = [4mnM/(mn+M)²]T. Natural Xe sums nine isotope spectra with different endpoints; the marker uses the abundance-weighted mean mass. The horizontal limit is 1.15 times that marker. The marker is a kinematic endpoint, not a guaranteed peak.
  • Lower left — partial waves: each integer ℓ is an orbital-angular-momentum component of the incoming wave (ℓ = 0, 1, 2 … are s, p, d …). Blue bars sum to the shape-elastic cross section; orange bars sum to optical reaction, not compound elastic. These are integrated cross sections, not angular curves. The phases between partial waves cause the angular interference and are not visible in the bars. kR is a rough scale for important waves, not a strict cutoff.
  • Lower right — potential: this shows the depth magnitudes versus distance from the nuclear centre. The real nuclear potential is attractive; the plotted positive blue depth is its magnitude. The absorptive depth combines volume and surface terms; the spin–orbit term used by the solver is not drawn. R is the potential's radius scale. The 1/k and 2π/k rulers compare reduced and full neutron wavelengths; their vertical placement has no physical meaning. For natural Xe this panel illustrates the mean-mass potential, while scattering is calculated isotope by isotope.

The black-sphere curve is a sharp absorption-limit toy model. The billiard-ball curve is a classical hard-sphere comparison, isotropic in the centre-of-mass frame. Neither replaces the optical model or a library evaluation.

ENDF minus JENDL: a fifth plot

The signed linear-scale plot below the four main panels subtracts JENDL-4.0u from ENDF/B-VII.1 at the same energy, isotope mixture and CM angle. It shows absolute elastic differential cross sections in b/sr, without dividing either curve by its elastic integral. A ratio would answer a different question.

Δ(θ) = (dσ/dΩ)ENDF/B-VII.1 − (dσ/dΩ)JENDL-4.0u
C = (σel,ENDF − σel,JENDL)/(4π) = (1/4π) ∫ Δ(θ) dΩ

The dashed line is the constant addition with the same integrated difference. For axial symmetry dΩ = 2π sin θ dθ; averaging uniformly over degrees gives the wrong angular weighting. The readout gives the solid-angle RMS of Δ−C over the full sphere. It describes angular variation, not a statistical uncertainty or a goodness-of-fit probability. Closeness to C must be judged relative to the accuracy needed and the display grid's interpolation limits.

A nearly horizontal difference is consistent with an isotropic change, but does not identify its physical mechanism. A sign-changing difference cannot be explained everywhere by adding a positive isotropic term alone. This diagnostic uses no optical-model subtraction and ignores the compound slider and overlay checkboxes. Energy and target controls update it continuously; axes stay fixed, with a separate manual difference-range selector and a notice when the curve is clipped.

What do the evaluated libraries provide?

The ENDF format identifies total cross section as MT=1 and elastic as MT=2. MT=3 denotes non-elastic when supplied; MT=4 is summed inelastic, MT=51–91 are its component levels/continuum, MT=102 is radiative capture, and MT=16 is (n,2n). Do not add an aggregate such as MT=4 to the levels it already contains. The loaded tool uses only total, elastic, and elastic angular distributions (MF=3 and MF=4); it does not import every reaction channel.

Legendre coefficients describe the angular distribution. They do not label shape versus compound mechanisms. The same final elastic angular curve can admit different model decompositions. Comparing two evaluations, or changing Legendre coefficients in a transport reweighting, does not by itself isolate compound elastic. The optical model shown here is an independently chosen baseline, not either evaluator's recovered internal calculation.

Saved OPTMAN calculations

OPTMAN is an optical-model solver that can explicitly couple nuclear excitation channels. It is a code, not a synonym for “shape.” These saved examples cover Xe-132 at two energies. The orange curve remains an assumption: no POD statistical-decay calculation is included.

With explicit direct inelastic channels, the bookkeeping is σtotal = σshape + σdirect inelastic + σabsorption. This panel assigns σcompound = fσabsorption, while σnon-elastic = σdirect inelastic + (1−f)σabsorption. Its denominator therefore differs from the spherical panel whenever direct excitation is nonzero.

The dotted orange curve shows the assumed component alone. For a single isotope it is constant both per unit solid angle and per unit recoil energy, up to the elastic endpoint. At zero it cannot appear on a logarithmic axis. This display neither runs Fortran nor interpolates OPTMAN to uncomputed energies.

Why 0.10 / 0.24 differ from 0.072 / 0.124

Matching the dip height and matching the total area are different conditions. These saved diagnostics use native evaluated curves and a direct spherical calculation for natural Xe at 2.45 MeV. They are more precise than the main slider grid.

farea = (σevaluated elastic − σshape) / σoptical reaction

flocal(θ) = 4π [(dσ/dΩ)evaluated − (dσ/dΩ)shape] / σoptical reaction

The solid-angle average of flocal equals farea. A truly isotropic difference would give a horizontal local-residual curve. A negative residual means this chosen shape model exceeds the evaluation there; it is not a negative physical compound cross section.

Thus 0.10 and 0.24 can help match parts of the dip while overshooting the integrated elastic cross section. These are model-dependent visual choices, not measured compound fractions. No paper confirming those two numbers as physical JENDL and ENDF compound fractions has been identified in the source review for this tool.

Coverage, precision, and sources

The shared bundle contains ENDF/B-VII.1 and the qualified JENDL-4.0u comparison, nine xenon isotopes, a 0.01–20 MeV optical grid, and the saved OPTMAN examples. The evaluated grid includes exact 2.45 and 14.1 MeV anchors and 1° angular samples. Energies within unreconstructed resolved or unresolved resonance ranges are masked. A mixture requires coverage for every isotope; unavailable isotopes are never silently dropped.

JENDL qualification: the Xe files are the NNDC JENDL-4.0 snapshot. JAEA's inspected update register lists no Xe update, supporting their use for this comparison, but a byte-for-byte comparison with the JENDL-4.0u archive has not been established. The visible “JENDL-4.0u” label retains this qualification.

The optical grid interpolates in log energy and log angular cross section. The evaluated display interpolates linearly in energy and cos θ, with discrete angular normalization. Deep minima between energy samples can have large relative errors; this is a teaching display, not a precision transport or dip-fitting database. Nonrelativistic kinematics use M = A × amu. The downloaded Colab notebook exposes the arrays and includes an optional direct spherical-model check; it does not contain raw ENDF inputs, resonance reconstruction, POD, or a Fortran compiler.
