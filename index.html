What the evaluated libraries say

A neutron that scatters once on a xenon nucleus gives it a recoil energy fixed by the scattering angle: forward scattering gives the smallest recoils and backward scattering the largest, up to about 74 keV at 2.45 MeV, the deuterium–deuterium (DD) neutron energy. The angular distribution is therefore the recoil spectrum. ENDF/B-VII.1 (red) and JENDL-4.0u (green) give different distributions; JEFF-4.0 (gold) can be added. Drag the energy or choose an isotope, and the plots redraw at once.

Why the libraries differ

They are built differently. JENDL-4.0u evaluates all nine stable xenon isotopes with one coupled-channel and statistical calculation. ENDF/B-VII.1 combines five older evaluations; its files for Xe-132, 134 and 136 (…% of natural xenon), taken from the Chinese evaluation CENDL-3, assemble a measured total cross section, an elastic cross section obtained by subtraction and a separately calculated angular shape.

Three files fail a physical bound. The optical theorem requires the forward elastic cross section to be at least (kσtotal/4π)², with k the centre-of-mass wave number. At 2.45 MeV those three ENDF/B-VII.1 files fall …% below it, so their total, elastic cross section and angular shape cannot come from one scattering amplitude. JENDL-4.0u and JEFF-4.0 satisfy the bound for every isotope (smallest ratios … and …). Passing does not prove a file right.

They cause most of the low-energy recoil difference. For one elastic scatter at 2.45 MeV, ENDF/B-VII.1 puts …% of recoils below 10 keV and JENDL-4.0u …%. Replacing only those three files by their JENDL-4.0u versions closes …% of that gap.

The difference is not a constant. The plot below the four main plots shows ENDF/B-VII.1 minus JENDL-4.0u. At 2.45 MeV it is neither a constant nor a cos θ term (…% and …% of its square integrated over solid angle): ENDF/B-VII.1 is lower at forward angles and higher by a near-constant … b/sr from …° to …° (recoils of … keV). The CENDL-3 files build …% of that stretch and the Xe-129 and Xe-131 files …% and …%.

A model for comparison

The blue and orange curves come from a calculation, not from a library. Shape elastic is the scattering by the average neutron–nucleus potential (Koning–Delaroche, spherical). Compound elastic, where the neutron is absorbed into a compound nucleus that then re-emits it, comes from a Hauser–Feshbach calculation (TALYS) with the same potential and has its calculated angular shape. The slider moves only within that calculation's range with a width-fluctuation correction: at 2.45 MeV, … of the reaction cross section, and almost nothing above … MeV.

The model is one potential. It does not show which library is right, and the difference between a library and the model's shape elastic is not compound elastic: it depends on the potential chosen and can be negative.

Details

Reading the plots
  • Angle: dσ/dΩ is the cross section per unit solid angle, in b/sr (1 b = 10⁻²⁸ m²), against the neutron's centre-of-mass scattering angle θ. Deep minima come from interference between partial waves.
  • Recoil: the same elastic events as xenon recoil energy, ER = Emax(1−cos θ)/2 and dσ/dER = (4π/Emax) dσ/dΩ for one isotope. Natural xenon sums nine isotopes with slightly different endpoints; the marker is the mean-mass endpoint. Dashed red lines mark minima of the model curve.
  • ENDF minus JENDL: the signed difference in b/sr, with the constant that has the same integrated area (dashed). Its readout gives the solid-angle RMS about that constant, a description, not an uncertainty.
  • Partial waves: each ℓ is an orbital-angular-momentum component of the wave. Blue bars sum to the shape-elastic cross section, orange bars to the cross section removed from the elastic wave. Integrating over angle removes the interference, so the bars cannot show where the minima are; kR is a rough scale for how many waves matter.
  • Potential: the real depth and the absorptive depth (volume plus surface) against distance from the nuclear centre; R is the radius and the arrow the reduced wavelength 1/k. For natural xenon the panel draws the mean-mass potential, while scattering is calculated isotope by isotope.

The optional black-sphere and billiard-ball curves are idealizations for contrast: a sharp absorbing sphere, and classical hard spheres scattering isotropically.

Cross-section accounting

σtotal = σelastic + σnon-elastic

σelastic = σshape + σcompound elastic

σreaction = σcompound elastic + σnon-elastic

Elastic leaves the nucleus in its original state; non-elastic is everything else: inelastic scattering to an excited state, capture with gamma emission, and other open channels. These plots show elastic recoils only.

Shape and compound describe how an elastic outcome is reached, not further final states. The model removes the reaction cross section from the elastic wave; part of it returns as compound elastic, the rest is non-elastic, so compound elastic moves cross section between the two groups and the total stays fixed. For natural xenon at 2.45 MeV the spherical calculation gives … b of shape elastic and … b of reaction cross section. A calculation that also follows the excitation of low-lying levels, such as the OPTMAN panel below, splits off that direct excitation first; a compound fraction means nothing until its denominator is stated.

The evaluated files store total, elastic and angular data (ENDF reaction identifiers MT=1 and MT=2, files 3 and 4). Their Legendre coefficients describe the angular distribution; they do not label shape or compound mechanisms.

A coupled-channel calculation for Xe-132 (OPTMAN)

OPTMAN is an optical-model code that can couple the excitation of low-lying levels to elastic scattering. These saved calculations cover Xe-132 at 2.45 and 14.1 MeV, with the Kunieda coupled-channel potential and, as a cross-check, the spherical Koning–Delaroche potential. Compound elastic is taken from the TALYS calculation and removed from the remaining absorption, so the total stays fixed.

Filling the minimum versus matching the area

An isotropic addition that fills a library's diffraction minimum over the model's shape elastic is not the one that matches the library's elastic cross section. The plots show, against angle, the isotropic fraction of the reaction cross section that would make the model equal each library there (solid), the value that matches the area (dashed) and the value that fills the minimum (dotted). The local value varies strongly and turns negative where the library lies below the model, so no single isotropic addition reproduces either library; a negative value is not a negative compound cross section.

farea = (σlibrary elastic − σshape) / σreaction

flocal(θ) = 4π [(dσ/dΩ)library − (dσ/dΩ)shape] / σreaction

None of these numbers is a measured or evaluated compound fraction; they are residuals of one chosen model.

Coverage, precision and sources

The shared bundle contains ENDF/B-VII.1, the qualified JENDL-4.0u comparison and JEFF-4.0 for the nine stable xenon isotopes, the spherical-model grid from 0.01 to 20 MeV, the TALYS compound-elastic range and shape, and the saved OPTMAN examples. The evaluated grid includes exact 2.45 and 14.1 MeV samples and 1° angles. Energies inside resonance ranges that are not reconstructed are masked, and a mixture needs every isotope.

JENDL qualification: the Xe files are the NNDC JENDL-4.0 snapshot. JAEA's update register lists no Xe update, and their angular coefficients reproduce those of the IAEA JENDL-4.0u conversion for Geant4 to 10⁻¹⁶ (orders up to 10, 1–3 MeV); byte identity with the 4.0u archive has not been established.

The model grid interpolates in log energy and log angular cross section; the evaluated display interpolates linearly in energy and cos θ. Deep minima between energy samples can have large relative errors: this is a teaching display, not a transport or fitting database. Non-relativistic kinematics use M = A × amu, except the forward-limit test above, which uses the relativistic wave number.
